# CRAFT.md — how to write well

**This file is the North Star of the skill.** Write by it. Clarity is
the job, and clarity is nearly all of the job.

VOICE.md is not a second layer applied on top of this one. It is a **tiebreaker**, opened at the
point where the passes below have produced more than one good version of a sentence and you need
to choose among them. Where the craft answer is singular — which is most of the time — you are
finished, and reaching for the voice profile can only make the sentence worse. When the two
conflict, **craft wins**: no voice marker justifies prose that is unclear, cluttered, padded, or
boring. (The exception, which is not stylistic: VOICE.md's fabrication guardrail and register
boundaries bind every sentence always.)

Grounded in William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (6th ed.), read in full. The rules and examples
below are his, condensed for use as working instructions; this file is a study aid for a book you
should own, not a substitute for it — **buy the book.** Where a rule cuts against what an AI writes by default, it is
marked **[against instinct]**.

---

## 0. The transaction (why any of this works)

The product a writer sells is not the subject but who they are. Out of that come the two qualities
worth chasing: humanity and warmth.

But personality on the page is **not a question of gimmicks to "personalize" the author. It is a
question of using the language with the greatest clarity and strength.** Clarity *is* the humanity.
They are the same work, not a trade-off, and that is why this file outranks the voice profile.

- **Care about the angle or change the angle.** If the writer is bored, the flatness transmits, and
  no sentence-level work will rescue it.
- **Enthusiasm is transferable; subject matter is not.** A reader with no interest in the topic
  stays if a person is unmistakably present and interested.

## 1. Before drafting: the structural decisions

Organizing the piece is the most untaught and underestimated skill in nonfiction. Clear sentences
fall apart without it. Do this work first.

**Answer six questions.** In what capacity am I addressing the reader? What pronoun and tense? What
style? What attitude toward the material? How much do I want to cover? **What one point do I want to
make?** The last two matter most.

**Hold three unities: pronoun, tense, mood.** Any tone is acceptable; mixing two or three is not.
Glancing backward or forward is what tenses are for, but switching the tense you principally address
the reader in is disqualifying.

**Think small. [against instinct]** The definitiveness complex is the belief that you owe the
subject the last word. There is no last word. Bite off one corner, cover it well, and stop. Tolstoy
did not write a book *about* war and peace. One man pursuing one whale. The second reason to reduce
scope is morale: an unwieldy task drains the writer's enthusiasm, and the reader notices your ebbing
zest before you do.

**Compress to human scale.** Nobody can write about the disappearance of small towns; it would be
all generalization and no humanity. Write about one town, then one store, then one family.

**Leave the reader with exactly one provocative thought they did not have before.** Not two.

**Gather far more material than you will use.** Every piece is strong in proportion to the surplus
you can choose from. Ackerman's two bat facts (one bat can register a beetle walking on sand;
another can detect a moth flexing its wings on a leaf) carry authority because of the hundred she
discarded.

## 2. The lead

The lead is a chain of tugs. Sentence one exists to get the reader to sentence two; if it fails, the
piece is dead, and the same is true of two to three. Length is not prescribable. The only test is
whether it pulls.

- **Open on a provocative idea, ideally one the reader has never had.** Zinsser on Timbuktu: the
  streets were sand, and sand is not dirt. Every town starts with dirt streets that get paved as
  people prosper and subdue their environment. Sand represents defeat. A city with streets of sand
  is a city at the edge.
- **One thought per sentence in the lead.** Plain declaratives, few commas. Readers process one idea
  at a time, in sequence.
- **Hook with** freshness, novelty, paradox, humor, surprise, an unusual idea, an interesting fact,
  or a question. Then give hard detail on why the piece exists, without dwelling.
- **Acknowledge what the reader already half knows.** It welcomes them as a fellow traveler.
- **Ration the background.** All of Timbuktu's history got three sentences.
- **Never use the breakfast-to-bed format.** The fishing trip does not open with the alarm clock.
- **Retire the stock leads:** the future archaeologist puzzling over our artifacts, the visitor from
  Mars, the thing that "just happened to happen one day not long ago," the have-in-common lead.
- **Consider just telling a story.** Narrative is the oldest way to hold attention.
- **The lead is over** when the necessary work is done and you can relax into the narrative.

**The first three paragraphs are often throwaway. [against instinct]** Editors routinely discard the
opening several paragraphs and start where the writer begins to sound like himself. Zinsser
describes a typical paragraph 1 as a tissue of generalities that could have come out of a machine,
that no *person* could have written. He wrote that in 1976 and it describes AI openings exactly.
Draft the lead, then check whether the piece actually starts three paragraphs down.

## 3. Sequence: the discipline that carries a technical subject

Writing is thinking on paper. Anyone who thinks clearly can write clearly about anything.

**Build the upside-down pyramid. [against instinct]** Start at the point with **the one fact the
reader must know before anything else can land.** The second sentence broadens the first; the third
broadens the second. Reach significance, implication, and speculation only after the narrow base is
set. This is the inverse of opening with importance, which is the default move.

**After every sentence, ask what the reader wants to know next, and answer that, not the question
you find most interesting. [against instinct]** Never write a paragraph without knowing where you
left the reader in the one before.

**Fact and deduction are the ruling family.** No fanciful leaps, no implied truths, no smooth
transition papering over a missing step in the causal chain.

**Assume the reader knows nothing**, including what "everybody knows" and what you explained earlier
in the same piece.

**Anchor every abstraction to something the reader can already picture.** The nautilus grows
gnomonically so its head does not get stuck. The cactus turns perpendicular to the light. A computer
is like a sophisticated pencil: you do not care how it works, but when it breaks you want someone
there to fix it. An inspector finding an obsolete technique still running: *suddenly we found a live
dinosaur.*

**Find the human element.** Use yourself as the handle, or hang the material on someone else's
story. Roueché does not open on the toxicology literature. He opens at eight on a September morning
with an old man collapsing on the sidewalk, taken for a drunk until it is clear he is not: his nose,
lips, ears and fingers were sky-blue.

**Keep technical vocabulary near zero.** Where a term is unavoidable, explain it in a clause. Your
responsibility is to the facts and to the reader, not to the vanity of an expert who would rather
sound impressive to his peers.

## 4. Sentences and words

**Verbs are the most important tool.** Active verbs push; passive verbs tug fitfully. A passive
style saps the reader's energy, because nobody quite knows what is being perpetrated by whom on
whom. Choose verbs whose imagery or sound carries meaning: glitter, dazzle, scatter, swagger, poke,
vex, bob. Be precise: did he step down, resign, retire, or get fired?

**Prefer short Anglo-Saxon words.** Words that sedate are three, four, and five syllables, mostly
Latinate, many ending in *-ion*, each embodying a vague concept. Of the 701 words in Lincoln's
Second Inaugural, 505 are one syllable.

**After verbs, plain nouns are the strongest tool**, because they resonate with emotion: leaves,
wind, frost, soil, labor, breath, courage, peace, home.

**Never let a concept noun do a verb's job. [against instinct]** *The common reaction is incredulous
laughter* has no people and no working verb. Turn it around: *most people just laugh with
disbelief*. A bag of abstract nouns sinks you to the bottom of the lake. The whole family is banned
as a default register: implementation, utilization, capabilities, functionality, optimization,
alignment, enablement.

**Never string nouns where one noun or verb will do.** Nobody goes broke now; we have money problem
areas. It no longer rains; we have precipitation activity.

**Cut adverbs that duplicate the verb** (blared loudly, clenched tightly) and **adjectives already
inside the noun** (precipitous cliffs, yellow daffodils). Every adjective must work: gray sky and
black clouds earn their place because the darkness is the *reason* for turning back. Not every oak
has to be gnarled. Sparse use is what keeps *drab* and *beautiful* powerful.

**Prune the little qualifiers.** A bit, a little, sort of, kind of, rather, quite, very, too, pretty
much, in a sense, arguably, **genuinely**, **truly**, **really**, **actually**. Every one whittles
away a fraction of the reader's trust. **Do not be kind of bold. Be bold.**

The test is subtraction: strike the word and read the sentence again. If it did not get weaker, the
word was doing nothing, and a word doing nothing is clutter. *It genuinely feels like the hard part
is over* is weaker than *it feels like the hard part is over*, because reaching for an intensifier
signals a claim the writer has not made concrete enough to stand on its own. Fix the claim, not the
adverb. (**"genuinely"** and **"honestly"** carry a further problem — staged candor presupposes
prior dishonesty — and many bylines soft-ban them outright; see VOICE.md Part 4.)

**Cut the clusters that announce the explaining. [against instinct]** Zinsser's clutter
chapter names them: *I might add*, *it should be pointed out*, *it is interesting to note*.
If you might add, add it. If it should be pointed out, point it out. If it is interesting,
make it interesting; being told that something is interesting is the surest way to tempt
the reader to find it dull. The spoken descendants are the same clutter with a microphone:
*I want to be careful here*, *here's the part that matters*, *worth sitting with*, *the real
question is*, *and that's the whole point*, *let me show you*. Every one is a note the
writer made to himself about what the next sentence should do, left in the text. **The test
is transplantation:** if the clause could be moved unchanged into a piece on another
subject, it carries nothing of this piece and it goes. (Added 2026-09-10; the rule was in
the book and in the wiki atom for it, and not in this file, which is how the same clause
reached three microphones in one month. Full category and the corpus evidence: LINT.md §12c.)

**Reach for the period sooner.** A hopelessly mired sentence usually holds two dissimilar thoughts.
Break it. There is no minimum sentence length.

**Signal a change of mood early. Start sentences with "But."** There is no stronger word at the
start, and the schoolroom prohibition is wrong. Never start a sentence with "However" (it hangs
there like a wet dishrag) and never end with it. Replace long summarizing clauses with one short
pivot: *Yet he decided to go.*

**Use "that" unless it makes the meaning ambiguous.** If the sentence needs a comma for its precise
meaning, it probably needs "which."

**Use contractions** where they fit comfortably; the uncontracted form is audibly stilted. Avoid
*I'd* (it means both *I had* and *I would*) and never invent *could've*.

**Avoid the exclamation point.** Get emphasis from word order. Never use one to flag a joke: readers
resent being told a moment was comical and are robbed of finding it funny themselves.

**On the dash:** Zinsser calls it invaluable and gives two structural uses. This project's default
house rule forbids em dashes in reader-facing prose — a detection-era concession rather than a
craft judgment, and yours to override in your VOICE.md. If you keep the ban, serve the underlying
purpose with a period, a comma pair, or parentheses.

## 5. Sound

Readers hear what they read far more than they realize, so rhythm is not decoration.

White's demonstration on Thomas Paine: *These are the times that try men's souls* survives
centuries. Rearranged (*Times like these try men's souls* / *These are trying times for men's souls*
/ *Soulwise, these are trying times*) it collapses into oatmeal. Same information, dead sentences.

- **Read everything aloud before it ships.** This catches what the eye forgives, and it is where
  clichés sound incriminating.
- **If all sentences move at the same plodding gait, that is the disease.** Three cures: reverse the
  order of a sentence, substitute a word with freshness or oddity, or alter lengths so they do not
  all sound as if they came from the same mold. **Any numeric length band in VOICE.md is descriptive
  evidence, not a target.** Targets converge, and convergence is the disease.
- **The occasional short sentence carries tremendous punch and stays in the reader's ear.** The
  effect depends entirely on scarcity, which is why a run of short sentences destroys the thing it
  imitates.
- **Reshape sentences so the last word is the one that stays in the ear.**
- **Load the last sentence of every paragraph.** It is the springboard into the next.
- **The paragraph is a unit of thought, not a rhythm device. [against instinct]** Zinsser
  (Bits & Pieces): short paragraphs invite the reader in, **but a succession of tiny
  paragraphs is as annoying as a paragraph that is too long** — the piece stops reading
  as a developed argument and starts reading as a series of announcements. A
  single-sentence paragraph is a spotlight. Cap for articles, essays, and chapters: **one
  per ~500 words, never two near each other.** If every third paragraph is one line, the
  piece is not emphatic; it is chopped. (Short-form dials in FORMATS.md — tweets,
  LinkedIn — have their own line-break conventions and override this.)

## 6. Trust your material (the highest-yield pass on generated prose)

The truth is more interesting than anything you can add.

- **Let a self-sufficient fact stand alone. [against instinct]** A scout with a stopwatch: a
  right-handed batter reaches first in 4.3 seconds, and the average double play takes 4.3 seconds.
  **No sentence is needed explaining that this is remarkably little time for a play involving one
  batted ball, two thrown balls and three infielders.** Given the number, readers do their own
  marveling, and they enjoy being allowed to think for themselves.
- **Cut *surprisingly*, *predictably*, *of course*, *notably*, *importantly*. [against instinct]**
  They stamp a value on a fact before the reader meets it, pre-empting a reaction that belongs to
  them.
- **Never over-explain.** Do not tell readers what they know or can surmise. **The reader plays a
  major role in the act of writing and must be given room to play it.**
- **Drop the bomb and say nothing.** State the striking fact and let it speak, with no notice
  that it was striking. *Worth sitting with*, *read that again*, *let that sink in*, *and that
  matters*, *why this matters* are the exclamation point with more syllables: they tell the
  reader a moment was remarkable and rob them of finding it so. If the fact needs the notice,
  the fact is not strong enough, and the notice will not save it.
- **Color is organic to the fact, not a separate ingredient.** A reporter whose lead was all *Whoosh!
  It was incredible* had buried the real specifics on page nine; asked why, he said "in the lead I
  was writing color." Reject the premise. Present the colorful fact.
- **Do not nudge the reader with significance.** Zinsser refused to make baseball a metaphor for lost
  youth. His premise was that baseball is a job, and he wanted to know how that job is taught.
- **Salvation lies not in style but in some odd fact you were able to discover.** He had a full
  afternoon of adequate research at the Baseball Hall of Fame, went back the next morning on
  instinct, and only then found the piece of slippery elm bark a pitcher chewed to load the spitball.
  The whole piece ended up standing on it.

## 7. Rewriting

Rewriting is the essence of the craft and where the game is won or lost. We have emotional equity in
the first draft and cannot believe it was not born perfect. The odds are near 100 percent that it
was not.

**Assume the new sentence has something wrong with it:** unclear, illogical, verbose, klunky,
pretentious, boring, cluttered, clichéd, unrhythmic, readable two ways, or not leading out of the
previous sentence. Rewriting is mostly reshaping and tightening, not producing whole new drafts.

### The passes, in order

1. **Cut.** Bracket every component doing no useful work and delete it. Bracket, too, every
   clause that could be transplanted unchanged into a different piece (§4): the bracket test
   finds words doing no work, the transplant test finds whole clauses doing none. **Most first
   drafts can be cut by 50 percent without losing any information or the author's voice.** (This figure is
   calibrated for articles. Book manuscripts run Mode B1 and preserve coverage — clutter goes,
   chapters do not. See the book dial in FORMATS.md.) Ask of every sentence:
   is every word doing new work? Can this be said with more economy? Is anything pompous or faddish?
   **Are you hanging on to something useless just because you think it is beautiful?**
2. **The quickest fix.** When an element resists every repair, ask whether you need it at all.
   Usually you do not. It was doing an unnecessary job all along, which is why it caused so much
   grief.
3. **Strengthen.** Upgrade verbs. Move the punch word to the end. Replace concept nouns with people
   doing things. Pair each abstraction with a particular.
4. **Check the joints. [against instinct]** *The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and
   malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions.* Nothing is wrong with sentence two alone; as a
   sequel it is very wrong, because the name in the reader's ear is Othello.
5. **Hunt clichés.** They are everywhere in the air around us, familiar friends ready to express
   complex ideas in shorthand, and **even careful writers use plenty on a first draft.** This pass is
   how taste gets applied.
6. **Read the whole thing aloud**, remembering where you left the reader in each previous sentence.
   This exposes where you lost him, confused him, failed to give him the one fact he needed, or told
   him the same thing twice.
7. **The spine test (house pass, added 2026-08-04 after a cohesion failure shipped).** The passes
   above are stated as judgments, and a judgment can be nodded through. This one produces an
   artifact, which cannot. For any essay, article, or chapter, before the checklist:
   - Write the one point in one sentence (§1 already demanded it; write it again now, from the
     finished draft, and check it is the same point).
   - **Walk the paragraphs top to bottom and write one clause per paragraph: the question the
     previous paragraph left in the reader's mind, and how this one answers it.** Do not skip
     paragraphs and do not write the clause from memory of what the paragraph should say.
   - A paragraph whose clause you cannot write is a skit — a self-contained bit with a punchline,
     connected to the piece only by theme. Weld it to the argument, move it to where its question
     actually gets asked, or cut it. **Pasting a transition sentence over the gap is not welding;**
     Zinsser's rule in §3 is that no smooth transition may paper over a missing step in the chain.
   - **For every paragraph whose first sentence opens with a pronoun or bare demonstrative** (it,
     this, that, they, he, she, "This is why..."), **name the antecedent out loud.** The antecedent
     must be the noun still in the reader's ear — normally the loaded last sentence of the previous
     paragraph. If naming it takes any thought at all, or the noun is three paragraphs back,
     replace the pronoun with the noun. This is pass 4's Othello/Iago rule applied at the joint
     where generated prose actually breaks: the paragraph boundary.
   - Count the single-sentence paragraphs against the §5 cap (one per ~500 words). Merge the rest
     back into the paragraphs that earned them.

**Do not swap a repeated word for a synonym by reflex. [against instinct]** Zinsser fought an editor
who "corrected" his deliberate repetition; he had used it because repetition takes readers by
surprise and refreshes them mid-sentence. He bought articles back from magazines over changes like
that.

**One sentence can be worth an hour.** He narrates nearly an hour on a single sentence, testing city
pairs, abandoning capitals for tourist sites, then hunting a fresher verb than *turn up* until he
found **bob**, a three-letter word that paints an object rising to the surface. **No writing decision
is too small to be worth a large expenditure of time.**

## 8. The ending

Readers who followed you through blind corners **will leave the moment the end is in sight**, because
an end in sight is a mirage.

- **Kill the summary ending. [against instinct]** (Refined for books: a chapter-end *tool* the
  reader operates from — a checklist, a question set, a decision table — is reference material,
  not a recap, and it stays. It is never the last thing on the page. See the book dial in
  FORMATS.md.) *In sum* / *To conclude* / *What insights have we
  gleaned* means you have arrived at Roman numeral III. You are about to repeat in compressed form
  what you already said in detail. Interest falters, the reader hears the cranking, and there is an
  implied insult that they were too dumb to get it the first time. **This includes bulleted "the
  short version" recaps.**
- **When you have presented the facts and made your point, look for the nearest exit.**
- **Aim for the curtain line.** Mid-scene, an actor says something funny, outrageous, or epigrammatic,
  and the lights go out. Startled, then delighted by the aptness.
- **Full circle works.** Echo a note sounded at the beginning.
- **You are under no obligation to the actual shape of events.** Zinsser cut his entire planned ending
  because writing it loomed as drudgery for him and the reader, which was the signal. The real climax
  was not the thing he traveled to find; it was a nomad family with almost nothing offering to share
  their dinner.
- **Give nearly as much thought to the last sentence as to the first.**

## 9. Writing for the ear (spoken scripts)

Everything above assumes silent reading. When the words will be spoken, the ear stops being a
diagnostic and becomes the medium.

- **One idea per sentence, subject and verb early.** No clause the listener must hold in memory to
  resolve. Roughly 8 to 16 words for most sentences, as a description of what works, not a quota.
- **Listeners cannot reread.** Information unfurls in order, once.
- **Signpost audibly and restate the key point once in different words.** This is the opposite of the
  written rule about distrusting transitions; in speech, repetition is load-bearing.
- **Emphasis replaces punctuation.** A short punch sentence where print would use a dash. A standalone
  line where print would italicize. A beat where print would use a colon.
- **Front-load.** Nobody is guaranteed to be there at minute two.
- **The binding test: read every line aloud.** If it trips the tongue, forces a breath mid-clause, or
  sounds *written*, rewrite it before the booth opens.

## 10. Humor, if it comes

Humor is a tool for making a point, not decoration. The mechanism is **heightening a real crazy truth
just enough that it is seen as crazy** — exposing lunacy that has become routine, not inventing it.

- **Never write the sermon.** *It's an outrage* is the death of humor. Find the comic frame that
  carries the point, or wait until you find one. Zinsser waited a year for his.
- **Control.** No comical invented names. Make the joke once. **Cut the extra sentence that explains a
  funny point already implicit.**
- **Do not strain.** Humor is built on surprise, and you can surprise a reader only so often.
- **Start from truth.** A student's funny piece about his grandmother's terrible cooking stopped being
  funny the moment he admitted she was a good cook.
- **Let humor sneak up so we hardly hear it coming.**

## The standard

There is no new knowledge in writing. Verbs have more vigor than nouns, active beats passive, short
beats long, concrete beats abstract. That has been settled since the King James Bible. Ninety percent
of the edge is the hard work of mastering the tools; a few points come from a good ear; the rest is
wanting to write better than everybody else and taking obsessive pride in the smallest details.

DiMaggio covered vast distances in the outfield in graceful strides, always arriving ahead of the
ball, making the hardest catch look routine. **He looked effortless because what he did could only be
achieved by great daily effort.** The same is true of prose that sounds like a man talking to you
from a rocking chair.
